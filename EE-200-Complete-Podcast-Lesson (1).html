LESSON 02 / 15

2. Complex numbers: two coordinates in one number

A complex number is written as “a plus i b,” where a and b are real numbers and i squared equals negative one. In many electrical engineering books the imaginary unit is called j because i is reserved for current. The arithmetic is exactly the same. Think of a plus i b as the ordered pair a, b: one coordinate along the real axis, and one along the imaginary axis.

On the board: z = a + ib ↔ (a, b); i² = −1; i³ = −i; i⁴ = 1; 1/i = −i.

The real part of z is a. The imaginary part is b, a real number, not “i b.” For z equal to three minus four i, the real part is three and the imaginary part is negative four. The conjugate flips the imaginary sign: a minus i b. Geometrically it reflects the point across the real axis. The magnitude is the length of the arrow from the origin: square root of a squared plus b squared. The magnitude can never be negative. It is zero only for the complex number zero.

On the board: Re(z) = a; Im(z) = b; z̄ = a − ib; |z| = √(a² + b²).

Let's complete the entire introductory table in the packet. For three minus four i, the conjugate is three plus four i and the magnitude is five, because nine plus sixteen equals twenty-five. For two plus one-half i, the imaginary part is one-half, the conjugate is two minus one-half i, and the magnitude is square root of seventeen over two: four plus one-quarter is seventeen-quarters. If the real part is negative five and the imaginary part is two, the number is negative five plus two i, its conjugate is negative five minus two i, and its magnitude is square root of twenty-nine. For negative seventeen i, the missing real part is zero, the conjugate is positive seventeen i, and the magnitude is seventeen. A number with zero real part is purely imaginary.

If you are given the conjugate, one minus i, conjugate it again to recover z equal to one plus i. Its magnitude is square root of two. And an ordinary real number such as twenty-nine is just twenty-nine plus zero i: its conjugate is itself and its magnitude is twenty-nine. More generally, the magnitude of a negative real number, such as negative twenty-nine, is positive twenty-nine.

On the board: 3−4i → (Re, Im, z̄, |z|) = (3, −4, 3+4i, 5).
2+i/2 → (2, 1/2, 2−i/2, √17/2).
−5+2i → (−5, 2, −5−2i, √29).
−17i → (0, −17, 17i, 17).
z̄=1−i → z=1+i, |z|=√2.
29 → (29, 0, 29, 29).

Try one. Let z equal negative two minus three i. Pause and name the imaginary part, conjugate, and magnitude. The answers are negative three, negative two plus three i, and square root of thirteen. If you answered “negative three i” for the imaginary part, remove the i: the imaginary part is a coordinate, not the whole imaginary term.

3. Arithmetic, conjugates, division, and geometry

Addition is coordinate-wise. Add real parts to real parts and imaginary parts to imaginary parts. Subtraction does the same thing after you distribute the minus sign to both terms in the second number. Multiplication needs the distributive rule. Expand all four products, then replace i squared by negative one. That last replacement is why a product of two imaginary terms contributes to the real part.

On the board: (a+ib) ± (c+id) = (a±c)+i(b±d).
(a+ib)(c+id) = (ac−bd)+i(ad+bc).

Let's do the five operations from the packet. Five i plus, in parentheses, negative nine plus i, is negative nine plus six i. Four plus twelve i minus, in parentheses, three minus fifteen i, is one plus twenty-seven i: subtracting negative fifteen i adds fifteen i. Seven i times negative five plus two i is negative thirty-five i plus fourteen i squared, or negative fourteen minus thirty-five i. One minus five i times negative nine plus two i has real pieces negative nine and positive ten, and imaginary pieces two i and forty-five i. The result is one plus forty-seven i. Finally, one minus eight i times one plus eight i equals one plus sixty-four, or sixty-five.

On the board: 5i+(−9+i)=−9+6i.
(4+12i)−(3−15i)=1+27i.
7i(−5+2i)=−14−35i.
(1−5i)(−9+2i)=1+47i.
(1−8i)(1+8i)=65.

The last pair were conjugates. In general, a plus i b times a minus i b equals a squared plus b squared, which is the magnitude squared. The imaginary cross terms cancel. That same cancellation explains two recovery formulas: add z and its conjugate, then divide by two, to get the real part. Subtract the conjugate from z, then divide by two i, to get the imaginary part.

On the board: zz̄ = a²+b² = |z|²; Re(z) = (z+z̄)/2; Im(z) = (z−z̄)/(2i).

When dividing complex numbers, the goal is a real denominator. Multiply numerator and denominator by the denominator's conjugate. This multiplies by one, so the quotient is unchanged. For a plus i b divided by c plus i d, the real denominator is c squared plus d squared. It can be zero only if the denominator was the zero complex number, which was already forbidden. Expanding the numerator gives real part a c plus b d and imaginary part b c minus a d, each divided by c squared plus d squared.

On the board: (a+ib)/(c+id) = (ac+bd)/(c²+d²) + i(bc−ad)/(c²+d²), for c+id ≠ 0.

Here are the four packet quotients, with the key numerator shown. Three minus i over two plus seven i: multiply by two minus seven i. The denominator is fifty-three, and the numerator is negative one minus twenty-three i. Answer: negative one over fifty-three minus twenty-three over fifty-three i. Three over nine minus i: multiply by nine plus i, giving twenty-seven plus three i over eighty-two. Eight i over one plus two i: multiply by one minus two i, giving sixteen plus eight i over five. Six minus nine i over two i: multiply by negative two i, giving negative eighteen minus twelve i over four, or negative nine-halves minus three i.

On the board: (3−i)/(2+7i)=−1/53−(23/53)i.
3/(9−i)=27/82+(3/82)i.
8i/(1+2i)=16/5+(8/5)i.
(6−9i)/(2i)=−9/2−3i.

Conjugation can pass through addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division: conjugate the inputs individually, then perform the operation; for division the denominator must be nonzero. This follows from the real-coordinate formulas, and it is a useful way to check an answer.

Now picture the plane. The horizontal coordinate is the real part; the vertical coordinate is the imaginary part. The magnitude is distance from the origin. Conjugation reflects over the horizontal axis. Addition places arrows tip to tail: the diagonal of the resulting parallelogram is their sum. This is why adding two complex numbers feels just like adding two two-dimensional vectors.

Practice pause: compute two plus i times one minus three i. The four products are two, negative six i, positive i, and negative three i squared. Replacing negative three i squared by positive three gives five minus five i. Its magnitude is five times square root of two. Its conjugate is five plus five i.

4. Polar form: lengths and turns

Rectangular form tells you how far right and up a point is. Polar form tells you how far it is from the origin and which direction it points. Write z as r times cosine theta plus i sine theta. The radius r is the magnitude. Theta, the argument, is measured in radians from the positive real axis. Since cosine and sine repeat every two pi, theta and theta plus two pi k describe the same nonzero complex number. Zero has magnitude zero but no unique direction, so its argument is undefined.

On the board: z = r(cos θ+i sin θ), r=√(a²+b²); a=r cos θ, b=r sin θ; θ is defined modulo 2π when z≠0.

To find theta, start with the quadrant. Tangent theta equals b over a when a is nonzero, but a calculator's inverse tangent only returns a principal angle between negative pi over two and positive pi over two. For a positive real part, the inverse tangent of b over a has the right direction; add two pi if you want a nonnegative angle in quadrant four. For a negative real part, add pi to the inverse tangent. On the positive imaginary axis the angle is pi over two. On the negative imaginary axis it is three pi over two, or equivalently negative pi over two. On the negative real axis it is pi. The safer mental method is always: find the reference angle, then put it in the actual quadrant. Many calculators offer “atan2” with both coordinates, which does this automatically.

The packet's first number is three plus three i. Its radius is square root of eighteen, or three square root of two, and its angle is pi over four. The second number is square root of three minus i. The square root sign matters: its radius is two and its quadrant-four angle is negative pi over six, equivalent to eleven pi over six. Reading the second number as plain three minus i would change every later product and power.

On the board: z₁=3+3i=3√2[cos(π/4)+i sin(π/4)].
z₂=√3−i=2[cos(−π/6)+i sin(−π/6)].

Why bother? Multiply two polar numbers and you multiply their radii and add their angles. Divide and you divide their radii and subtract their angles. You can see the multiplication rule by expanding the two parentheses: the real component becomes cosine theta one cosine theta two minus sine theta one sine theta two, which is cosine of their sum. The imaginary component is the corresponding sine addition identity. Geometrically, multiplication stretches and rotates; division rescales and rotates in the opposite direction.

On the board: z₁z₂ = r₁r₂[cos(θ₁+θ₂)+i sin(θ₁+θ₂)].
z₁/z₂ = (r₁/r₂)[cos(θ₁−θ₂)+i sin(θ₁−θ₂)], z₂≠0.

For the packet's two numbers, the product radius is six square root of two and its angle is pi over twelve. You do not have to memorize sine and cosine at pi over twelve to give rectangular form: multiply three plus three i by square root of three minus i directly. The real part is three square root of three plus three; the imaginary part is three square root of three minus three. For z one divided by z two, the angle is five pi over twelve. Rationalize by square root of three plus i: the denominator is four, giving real part, three square root of three minus three, over four; and imaginary part, three square root of three plus three, over four. In the reverse direction, z two divided by z one has angle negative five pi over twelve, and rectangular form is square root of three minus one over six, minus i times square root of three plus one over six.

On the board: z₁z₂=(3√3+3)+i(3√3−3).
z₁/z₂=(3√3−3)/4+i(3√3+3)/4.
z₂/z₁=(√3−1)/6−i(√3+1)/6.

Pause and estimate before calculating: z one points forty-five degrees up, and z two points thirty degrees down. Their product must point fifteen degrees up. If your imaginary part came out negative, you would know to revisit the arithmetic before trusting the answer.

5. Powers, roots, and Euler's formula

Repeated multiplication leads to de Moivre's theorem. If z has radius r and angle theta, z to an integer power n has radius r to the n and angle n theta. Negative powers require z nonzero. For z one equal to three plus three i, the fifth-power radius is nine hundred seventy-two square root of two. Five times pi over four points to quadrant three, where sine and cosine are both negative square root of two over two. Multiplying gives negative nine hundred seventy-two minus nine hundred seventy-two i. For z two equal to square root of three minus i, raising radius two to negative eight gives one over two hundred fifty-six. Multiplying the angle negative pi over six by negative eight gives four pi over three. Cosine there is negative one-half; sine is negative square root of three over two. The answer is negative one over five hundred twelve, minus square root of three over five hundred twelve i.

On the board: [r(cos θ+i sin θ)]ⁿ=rⁿ[cos(nθ)+i sin(nθ)].
(3+3i)⁵=−972−972i.
(√3−i)⁻⁸=−1/512−(√3/512)i.

Roots reverse the power process, but an important choice appears. If w to the n equals a nonzero z with angle theta, the radius of w must be the positive nth root of r. Its angle can be theta over n, or theta plus one full turn over n, or theta plus two full turns over n, and so on. Exactly n of those directions are distinct. Label them k equals zero through n minus one. Each lies on a circle with radius r to the power one over n, and neighboring roots are two pi over n radians apart. Zero is the special case: its only distinct nth root is zero.

On the board: wₖ=r¹/ⁿ[cos((θ+2πk)/n)+i sin((θ+2πk)/n)], k=0,1,…,n−1, for r>0.

Solve x to the fifth minus thirty-two equals zero. First rewrite it as x to the fifth equals thirty-two. The target radius is thirty-two, its angle is zero, and the fifth-root radius is two. The five answers are two times cosine of two pi k over five plus i sine of two pi k over five, with k from zero through four. Spell out the angles: zero, two pi over five, four pi over five, six pi over five, and eight pi over five. The first root is the familiar real solution two; the other four are equally important complex answers.

On the board: x⁵=32 → xₖ=2[cos(2πk/5)+i sin(2πk/5)], k=0,…,4.

Now x to the sixth plus sixty-four equals zero means x to the sixth equals negative sixty-four. The target angle is pi, not zero. The root radius is two and the six angles are pi over six, pi over two, five pi over six, seven pi over six, three pi over two, and eleven pi over six. In rectangular form the six answers are square root of three plus i; two i; negative square root of three plus i; negative square root of three minus i; negative two i; and square root of three minus i. Check the first one: its radius is two, and multiplying its thirty-degree angle by six yields one hundred eighty degrees. Its sixth power is negative sixty-four.

On the board: x⁶=−64 → xₖ=2[cos((π+2πk)/6)+i sin((π+2πk)/6)], k=0,…,5.
Rectangular roots: √3+i, 2i, −√3+i, −√3−i, −2i, √3−i.

Euler's formula packages the entire unit circle into an exponential: e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta. Consequently r e to the i theta is polar form in one compact expression. For any real a and b, e to the a plus i b equals e to the a times cosine b plus i sine b. Its magnitude is e to the a; its argument is b modulo two pi. Exponentials still satisfy e to the z one plus z two equals e to the z one times e to the z two. They are periodic in the imaginary direction: adding two pi i to the exponent does not change the value.

On the board: eⁱˣ=cos x+i sin x; eᵃ⁺ⁱᵇ=eᵃ(cos b+i sin b); eᶻ¹⁺ᶻ²=eᶻ¹eᶻ²; eᶻ⁺²πⁱ=eᶻ.

Write Euler's formula once for x and once for negative x. Adding those equations cancels sine, giving cosine x equals e to the i x plus e to the negative i x, divided by two. Subtracting them cancels cosine, giving sine x equals e to the i x minus e to the negative i x, divided by two i. The same expressions define cosine and sine for complex inputs. For a quick proof of the angle-addition formulas with real angles, multiply e to the i z one and e to the i z two. Group the real part and the coefficient of i. The real part is cosine z one cosine z two minus sine z one sine z two; the imaginary part is sine z one cosine z two plus cosine z one sine z two. Those are the cosine and sine of z one plus z two. For complex inputs the identities still hold, as you can verify by substituting their exponential definitions; comparing ordinary real and imaginary parts alone would not justify that extension.

On the board: cos z=(eⁱᶻ+e⁻ⁱᶻ)/2; sin z=(eⁱᶻ−e⁻ⁱᶻ)/(2i).
cos(z₁+z₂)=cos z₁ cos z₂−sin z₁ sin z₂.
sin(z₁+z₂)=sin z₁ cos z₂+cos z₁ sin z₂.

Here is a separate check of the packet's remaining identities. Square the two exponential expressions for sine and cosine and add. Since one over i squared is negative one, the exponential-square terms cancel and the result is one: sine squared z plus cosine squared z equals one. For the double-angle sine formula, two sine x cosine x becomes the difference of e to the two i x and e to the negative two i x, divided by two i: exactly sine of two x. For cosine, two cosine squared x minus one becomes the sum of those same two exponentials divided by two: cosine of two x. The identity uses squares of the function values, not “sine of x squared.”

On the board: sin²z+cos²z=1; sin(2x)=2 sin x cos x; cos(2x)=2 cos²x−1.

Practice pause: what is e to the negative two plus i pi? The radius is e to the negative two. Cosine pi is negative one and sine pi is zero. The answer is negative e to the negative two, an ordinary negative real number.

6. Hyperbolic functions: exponentials with a different geometry

Hyperbolic cosine, pronounced “kosh,” and hyperbolic sine, pronounced “sinch,” are built from real exponentials. Cosh x is e to the x plus e to the negative x, divided by two. Sinh x is e to the x minus e to the negative x, divided by two. Adding cosh and sinh gives e to the x; subtracting gives e to the negative x. Both definitions also extend to complex inputs.

On the board: cosh x=(eˣ+e⁻ˣ)/2; sinh x=(eˣ−e⁻ˣ)/2.
cosh x+sinh x=eˣ; cosh x−sinh x=e⁻ˣ.

Compare with the exponential formulas for cosine and sine. Substituting i x into cosine gives cosh x. Substituting i x into sine gives i sinh x, so sinh x equals negative i times sine of i x. In the other direction, cosh of i x is cosine x, and sinh of i x is i sine x. You can verify any of these by inserting i x into the exponential definition and using i squared equals negative one.

On the board: cos(ix)=cosh x; sinh x=−i sin(ix); cosh(ix)=cos x; sinh(ix)=i sin x.

Square cosh and subtract the square of sinh. The exponential squares cancel, leaving one. This looks like the circle identity, but with a minus sign: cosine squared plus sine squared equals one; cosh squared minus sinh squared equals one. As t changes, the point with coordinates cosh t, sinh t lies on the right-hand branch of the hyperbola X squared minus Y squared equals one. The lecture packet calls this a “parabola”; the equation describes a hyperbola. The familiar pair cosine t, sine t traces the unit circle.

On the board: cosh²t−sinh²t=1 → X²−Y²=1 for (X,Y)=(cosh t,sinh t).
cos²t+sin²t=1 → X²+Y²=1 for (X,Y)=(cos t,sin t).

Differentiate the definitions. The derivative of sinh x is cosh x, and the derivative of cosh x is sinh x. Notice there is no negative sign in the second derivative. With a constant a not equal to zero, reverse the chain rule to integrate: the integral of sinh of a x is cosh of a x divided by a, plus C; the integral of cosh of a x is sinh of a x divided by a, plus C. The packet's printed integral line drops an a in one place; check by differentiating the answers. If a equals zero, integrate the resulting constant functions separately.

On the board: (sinh x)'=cosh x; (cosh x)'=sinh x.
∫sinh(ax) dx=cosh(ax)/a+C; ∫cosh(ax) dx=sinh(ax)/a+C, a≠0.

Pause here. We are about to move from numbers to unknown functions. The same habit of testing domains and checking equations will carry us through.

7. Why differential equations appear in circuits

Imagine one loop containing a source, a resistor, and an inductor. A resistor has resistance R in ohms, and its voltage drop is R times current I. An inductor has inductance L in henries; its voltage drop is L times the rate of change of current. If the source voltage is E of t volts, Kirchhoff's voltage law says the signed changes around the loop sum to zero. With the usual passive sign convention, the two component drops add to the source: L times I prime plus R I equals E of t. Current is in amperes and time in seconds.

On the board: E_R=RI; E_L=L(dI/dt); E_L+E_R=E(t) → L(dI/dt)+RI=E(t).

Now replace the inductor with a capacitor of capacitance C in farads. Charge q is measured in coulombs. The capacitor voltage is q divided by C, and current is charge's rate of change, d q over d t. Kirchhoff's law gives R times I plus q over C equals the source. Substitute I equals d q over d t, and you get R times q prime plus q over C equals E of t. These two circuit equations are first-order differential equations because the unknown is a function of time and its first derivative appears.

On the board: E_C=q/C; I=dq/dt; RI+q/C=E(t) → R(dq/dt)+q/C=E(t).

A differential equation is an equation involving derivatives of an unknown function. In I prime plus three I equals sine of one hundred t, I is the dependent variable and t is the independent variable. In y double prime minus two y over x equals zero, y depends on x. The order is the highest derivative appearing, not the largest exponent anywhere else. The first circuit equation is first order, even though it may contain a sine with a large number in its argument.

A proposed solution must work on an interval: substitute it and its necessary derivatives into the original equation, and require equality at every point of that interval. Try y equals two minus three e to the negative four x in y prime plus four y equals eight. Its derivative is twelve e to the negative four x. Adding four y gives twelve e to the negative four x plus eight minus twelve e to the negative four x, exactly eight. The function is defined for every real x, so it works on the entire real line.

For the packet's second check, y equals two e to the negative x minus x e to the negative x. Differentiate with the product rule: y prime is negative three e to the negative x plus x e to the negative x. Differentiate again: y double prime is four e to the negative x minus x e to the negative x. In y double prime plus two y prime plus y, the ordinary e to the negative x coefficients are four minus six plus two, or zero. The x e to the negative x coefficients are negative one plus two minus one, also zero. This is indeed a solution of that second-order equation for all real x.

On the board: y=2−3e⁻⁴ˣ → y'+4y=12e⁻⁴ˣ+4(2−3e⁻⁴ˣ)=8.
y=(2−x)e⁻ˣ → y'=(x−3)e⁻ˣ, y''=(4−x)e⁻ˣ → y''+2y'+y=0.

An initial value problem gives the differential equation and values at a starting point. Think of the equation as a rule for motion and the initial values as the starting position and, for a second-order equation, its starting slope. Consider y double prime plus y prime minus two y equals zero, with y at zero equal to two and y prime at zero equal to one. The packet proposes the family c one e to the x plus c two e to the negative two x. Its first derivative is c one e to the x minus two c two e to the negative two x. Its second derivative is c one e to the x plus four c two e to the negative two x. Substitution cancels both exponential coefficients, so every member solves the equation.

At x equals zero, the first condition says c one plus c two equals two. The slope condition says c one minus two c two equals one. Subtract the second equation from the first: three c two equals one. Thus c two is one-third and c one is five-thirds. Our particular solution is five-thirds e to the x plus one-third e to the negative two x. Substituting x equals zero checks the value two, and differentiating checks the slope one.

On the board: y''+y'−2y=0, y(0)=2, y'(0)=1.
y=c₁eˣ+c₂e⁻²ˣ → c₁+c₂=2, c₁−2c₂=1 → c₁=5/3, c₂=1/3.
y=(5/3)eˣ+(1/3)e⁻²ˣ.

A general solution is a family with arbitrary constants. A particular solution chooses constants, often by initial conditions. The notes also introduce singular solutions: solutions lost by a step such as division. For example, y prime equals two square root of y has the constant solution y equals zero. Separating on a region where y is positive produces parabolic branches y equals, in parentheses, x minus C, squared, but only where x minus C is nonnegative: the square root of a square is an absolute value. In fact, you can stay at zero up to some time C and then follow the positive parabola afterward. This is a useful reminder that the zero solution and such joined solutions require direct checking in the original equation; a bare parabola on the whole real line is not automatically valid.

On the board: y'=2√y admits y≡0. For any C, y(x)=0 for x≤C and y(x)=(x−C)² for x≥C is another solution.

8. Classify before choosing a method

An ordinary differential equation has one independent variable and ordinary derivatives. The packet gives examples involving y and x, or a current and time, all of which are ODEs even when fourth derivatives or nonlinear functions appear. A partial differential equation has a function of more than one independent variable and partial derivatives. The heat equation says u sub t equals kappa times u sub x x; u depends on time and position. Laplace's equation says u sub x x plus u sub y y equals zero; u depends on two spatial variables. The packet also lists a wave equation, u sub t t equals alpha squared times u sub x x, and a radial equation, N sub t equals N sub r r plus one over r times N sub r plus k N. These examples distinguish the notation, not methods we will solve in this episode.

On the board: ODE: y''−(2/x)y=0. PDE: uₜ=κuₓₓ; uₓₓ+uᵧᵧ=0; uₜₜ=α²uₓₓ; Nₜ=Nᵣᵣ+(1/r)Nᵣ+kN.

Order means the order of the highest derivative. y prime plus y cubed equals sine y is first order. If the third derivative is squared and the first derivative is raised to the fifth power, the equation is third order; an algebraic exponent does not turn a first derivative into a fifth derivative. An equation with a fourth derivative is fourth order. A PDE containing u sub x x and a cube of u sub y is second order. A standard nth-order initial value problem usually specifies y at x zero and derivatives through order n minus one there. This count is the familiar rule for a regular nth-order equation; special or singular equations require checking rather than assuming those conditions guarantee a solution.

On the board: order[(y''')²+4(y')⁵=1+y²] = 3; order[y⁽⁴⁾−3y'''+y³+eʸ=7 tan x] = 4; order[uₓₓ−(uᵧ)³=x−2y] = 2.

Linear is a separate question from order. An nth-order linear ODE can be written as a sum of coefficients depending on x times y and its derivatives, each appearing to the first power, equal to a function of x. The unknown cannot be inside sine, an exponential, or a square; derivatives cannot multiply each other. The coefficient of y may be sine x or x squared, because x is already known. In the packet, y double prime minus five y equals sine of y is nonlinear because sine acts on the unknown. For the first-order equations in these notes, our first diagnostic is separability: can y prime be factored into a function of x times a function of y? One more vocabulary pair. An explicit solution names y as a function of x. An implicit solution gives a relation F of x and y equals a constant, whose branches may define explicit functions. 11. First-order linear equations and the integrating factor

13. Exact equations: find the hidden potential function

Write an equation as M of x and y d x plus N of x and y d y equals zero. Suppose there is a function F of x and y whose partial derivative with respect to x is M and whose partial derivative with respect to y is N. Then M d x plus N d y is just d F. Along a solution, d F equals zero, so F stays constant. This makes F equal to C an implicit solution. Under the usual smoothness and region assumptions, a practical test is partial M with respect to y equals partial N with respect to x. Say it aloud as “M sub y equals N sub x.” The derivative indices are crossed because both are mixed second derivatives of F.

On the board: M dx+N dy=0. If Fₓ=M and Fᵧ=N, then dF=0 and F(x,y)=C.
Exactness test: Mᵧ=Nₓ on a suitable region.

Let's classify the packet's two examples. In y to the fourth minus two plus four x y cubed y prime equals zero, the d x coefficient is M equal to y to the fourth minus two; the d y coefficient is N equal to four x y cubed. M sub y and N sub x both equal four y cubed, so the equation is exact. In y squared plus two x y times y prime plus x squared plus two x y equals zero, put the y-prime coefficient with d y: M is x squared plus two x y, and N is y squared plus two x y. M sub y is two x; N sub x is two y. They are not equal throughout a two-dimensional region, so this particular differential form is not exact.

On the board: (y⁴−2)dx+4xy³dy=0: Mᵧ=Nₓ=4y³, exact.
(x²+2xy)dx+(y²+2xy)dy=0: Mᵧ=2x, Nₓ=2y, not exact.

Now solve the first example. Integrate M with respect to x, keeping y fixed: F equals x y to the fourth minus two x plus an unknown function g of y. Why g of y, not just a numerical constant? When you differentiate with respect to x, any function of y disappears. Differentiate this candidate F with respect to y: four x y cubed plus g prime of y. It has to equal N, which is exactly four x y cubed, so g prime is zero. Absorb the resulting numerical constant into C. Thus x y to the fourth minus two x equals C.

You can also integrate both partial derivatives as the lecture method requests. Integrating M in x gives x y to the fourth minus two x plus g of y. Integrating N in y gives x y to the fourth plus h of x. Compare the common x y to the fourth. The missing x-only part in the second expression is negative two x, so h of x is negative two x; the y-only part g is a constant. Both routes produce the same F. Differentiate F to check: F sub x equals y to the fourth minus two and F sub y equals four x y cubed.

On the board: ∫(y⁴−2)dx=xy⁴−2x+g(y).
∫4xy³dy=xy⁴+h(x).
Match → F=xy⁴−2x; answer: xy⁴−2x=C.

Next, the exact initial value problem: y minus one over x times y prime equals x minus y over x squared, with y at one equal to two. The original equation requires x nonzero. Move the right side to the left and put the y-prime coefficient with d y. M equals y over x squared minus x; N equals y minus one over x. M sub y and N sub x both equal one over x squared, so this is exact on either half-plane x positive or x negative. Because the initial x is one, choose the positive side.

Integrate M in x, treating y as fixed. The integral of y over x squared is negative y over x, and the integral of negative x is negative x squared over two. So F equals negative y over x minus x squared over two plus g of y. Differentiate with respect to y: negative one over x plus g prime of y must equal y minus one over x. Therefore g prime is y, and g equals y squared over two. To check from the other direction, integrate N in y: y squared over two minus y over x plus h of x. Matching gives h equals negative x squared over two. Thus F equals one-half y squared minus y over x minus one-half x squared.

At x one, y two, F equals two minus two minus one-half, or negative one-half. Multiply F equals negative one-half by two: y squared minus two times y over x minus x squared equals negative one. Complete the square in y by adding one over x squared to both sides. The left side becomes the square of the whole quantity y minus one over x. The right side becomes x squared minus one plus one over x squared. Take square roots. At x one, the center one over x equals one and the square root equals one. To get y equal to two, choose the plus sign. The answer is one over x plus the square root of the whole quantity x squared minus one plus one over x squared, for x positive. The expression under the root is positive for every x positive: after multiplying by x squared it becomes x to the fourth minus x squared plus one, which equals the square of x squared minus one-half, plus three-quarters. Thus the branch is defined throughout x greater than zero. Check both the initial condition and the original equation if you work this one on paper.

On the board: (y−1/x)y'=x−y/x², y(1)=2, x>0.
M=y/x²−x; N=y−1/x; Mᵧ=Nₓ=1/x².
F=y²/2−y/x−x²/2=C; C=−1/2.
(y−1/x)²=x²−1+1/x².

Pause and notice the roles of our methods. A linear integrating factor modifies a standard-form linear equation so the left side is one product derivative. An exact-equation integrating factor modifies the whole differential form so it becomes the total differential of F. The word “integrating factor” appears in both, but the diagnostic formulas and algebra look different.

14. When the differential form is not exact

Suppose M d x plus N d y equals zero and M sub y differs from N sub x. We can try to multiply the entire equation by a nonzero function mu. If mu depends only on x, exactness of mu M and mu N says mu M sub y equals mu prime N plus mu N sub x. Rearrange and divide where N and mu are nonzero: mu prime over mu equals, M sub y minus N sub x, over N. If that final ratio depends only on x, integrate it with respect to x and exponentiate to construct mu of x. If the ratio still contains y, this particular test fails.

If mu depends only on y, exactness says mu prime M plus mu M sub y equals mu N sub x. Thus mu prime over mu equals, N sub x minus M sub y, over M. If that ratio depends only on y, integrate it with respect to y and exponentiate. These are useful sufficient tests for factors of one variable. M sub y is zero; N sub x is negative one over y. Use the y-only test: N sub x minus M sub y, divided by M, is positive one over y. Exponentiate the integral log absolute y. On either fixed-sign region you may choose mu equal to y; any nonzero constant multiple works. The multiplied equation is negative y d x plus, in parentheses, y sine y minus x, d y equals zero. Its crossed partial derivatives are both negative one.

Integrate negative y with respect to x to get F equals negative x y plus g of y. Differentiate with respect to y: negative x plus g prime must equal y sine y minus x. Thus g prime equals y sine y. Integration by parts gives g equals negative y cosine y plus sine y. Therefore negative x y minus y cosine y plus sine y equals a constant. Multiplying the entire equation by negative one and renaming the constant gives the equally valid form x y plus y cosine y minus sine y equals C. Keep y nonzero: the transformed equation admits the line y equals zero for a suitable constant, but the original has x over y and never defines that line.

15. Exam rehearsal: pause, solve, then compare

We have completed the packet. Now use the next short prompts as a rehearsal. For each one, pause, name the method before doing the algebra, and compare both the result and its domain. A wrong first step is easier to catch when you have named what kind of equation you are solving.

First: what is the imaginary part of negative four plus seven i, and what is its magnitude? The imaginary part is seven, without i. The magnitude is square root of sixteen plus forty-nine, or square root of sixty-five. The conjugate, if you want an extra check, is negative four minus seven i.

Second: simplify one plus i over one minus i. Multiply by one plus i over one plus i. The numerator is one plus two i plus i squared, or two i; the denominator is one minus i squared, or two. The quotient is i. As a polar check, the numerator has angle plus forty-five degrees and the denominator minus forty-five degrees, so the quotient has angle ninety degrees and magnitude one.

Third: how many distinct fourth roots does negative sixteen have, and where are they? Four. Its radius is sixteen and angle pi. Root radius two; angles pi over four, three pi over four, five pi over four, and seven pi over four. The rectangular roots are square root of two plus i square root of two, negative square root of two plus i square root of two, negative square root of two minus i square root of two, and square root of two minus i square root of two. The real fourth root does not exist, but the complex roots do.

Fourth: solve y prime equals x times y squared, recording anything division might lose. For y nonzero, y to negative two d y equals x d x; integrate to negative one over y equals x squared over two plus C. Rearranging gives y equals negative one over x squared over two plus C, on intervals without a zero denominator. The constant solution y equals zero must be added separately. You can verify each answer directly.

Fifth: classify y prime plus three over x times y equals x squared for x positive. It is first-order, linear, and ordinary. Its integrating factor is x cubed. Multiplying gives the derivative of x cubed y equals x to the fifth. Integrate to x cubed y equals x to the sixth over six plus C, so y equals x cubed over six plus C over x cubed. This example tests whether you normalize the equation and multiply every term.

Sixth: solve y prime plus two y equals x y squared, and check a lost solution. It is Bernoulli with n two. Let u equal one over y on a nonzero branch. Then u prime minus two u equals negative x. An integrating factor is e to negative two x. Alternatively, look for a particular u equal A x plus B: A minus two A x minus two B equals negative x, so A is one-half and one-half minus two B is zero, giving B one-quarter. The general u is one-half x plus one-quarter plus C e to the two x. Thus y is its reciprocal wherever defined, and y equals zero also solves the original equation. Substitute u back if the signs feel surprising.

Seventh: the differential form two x y d x plus x squared d y equals zero is exact. M sub y and N sub x both equal two x. The potential is x squared y. Therefore x squared y equals C. On x nonzero this is y equals C over x squared. At x zero, the differential form degenerates, so keep an interval and interpretation consistent with the original equation.

Eighth: a quick circuit check. If the source voltage is zero in an RL circuit, L I prime plus R I equals zero. Separate or use the linear integrating factor: I of t equals I at zero times e to the negative R over L times t. The ratio L over R has units of time and is the decay time constant. Differentiate: I prime equals negative R over L times I, so L I prime plus R I equals zero.

Here is the final checklist to carry into the exam. For complex arithmetic, separate the real and imaginary coefficients, remember i squared, and keep the denominator real. In polar problems, get the quadrant before the angle; for roots, include all n directions. For differential equations, identify the original domain, classify the form, check solutions lost through division, apply initial data to the right branch, and substitute your result back. When using an exact-equation factor, multiply both M and N, check the crossed partial derivatives again, and restrict away from points where the original equation was undefined.

You do not need to guess what an equation “looks like.” A method is a short sequence of justified steps. If you can say aloud why the next step is allowed, you can reconstruct it under exam pressure. That is the whole point of this review.
